# Design

The editable [Controller](https://www.figma.com/design/nsTQnUj3rHsi5tY68xGoXW?node-id=5-10), [150% controller](https://www.figma.com/design/nsTQnUj3rHsi5tY68xGoXW?node-id=10-77), and [Full history](https://www.figma.com/design/nsTQnUj3rHsi5tY68xGoXW?node-id=10-43) were updated with quieter controls, centered results, sound controls and ordinal history labels. The [guide chooser](https://www.figma.com/design/nsTQnUj3rHsi5tY68xGoXW?node-id=19-84) and [sound interaction notes](https://www.figma.com/design/nsTQnUj3rHsi5tY68xGoXW?node-id=18-90) document the new behavior. The connected Figma font catalog does not include Aeonik, so editable Figma text retains Inter; the application continues to embed Aeonik. Figma is a layout specification, not a running audio or WebGL prototype.

WhizReveal brings a sense of depth to a focused raffle workflow. The operator configures a pool on the left, runs and previews draws in the center, and reviews session history on the right. Text-size controls sit with the controller's global actions so the operator can adjust readability without changing the audience presentation.

The editable screens and design foundations are in the [WhizReveal Figma file](https://www.figma.com/design/nsTQnUj3rHsi5tY68xGoXW).

The v1.2 visual direction takes inspiration from [Lusion](https://lusion.co/): generous space, an interactive sculptural object, rounded utility actions, and a clear typographic hierarchy. A locally generated torus-knot mesh responds gently to the pointer. Its shader shades the form from the existing five-color palette, with no downloaded model or texture. The sculpture moves into the background once a draw exists. The surrounding controls use simpler surfaces and fewer decorative borders, shadows, icons, and hints.

Utility actions such as Guide, Launch display, and New session use compact uppercase styling. Labels and longer text use sentence case; the WhizReveal wordmark retains its spelling. Guide opens a language chooser followed by an accessible, scrollable dialog containing English, Simplified Chinese, or Indonesian instructions. Escape closes it and restores focus. The controller's Enter shortcut is blocked while any dialog is open.

## Color and hierarchy

The design uses five color tokens throughout, plus one text-only derivative:

| Token | Color | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canvas | `#0B0D10` | Main background and deepest surfaces |
| Surface | `#171C22` | Panels and controls |
| Edge | `#2A333D` | Borders and structural depth |
| Muted | `#5D6F82` | Borders, accents, icons, decorative text and disabled controls |
| Primary | `#E7EEF5` | Numbers, readable labels, and highlights |
| Muted text (derived, text only) | `#808F9F` | Informative secondary text: hints, captions, section labels, counts, times, dialog body copy |

Muted text is Muted moved a quarter of the way toward Primary (`--color-muted-text` in `src/styles.css`; `MUTED_TEXT` in `src/lib/color-contrast.ts`, whose tests check the stylesheet against it). Muted itself reaches only 3.3:1 on Surface and 3.8:1 on Canvas, below the WCAG AA 4.5:1 for normal text; Muted text reaches 5.2:1 on Surface and 5.9:1 on Canvas while staying clearly quieter than Primary (14.6:1 on Surface). It is never used for fills, borders or icons. Muted remains for text that is decorative (the baseline arrow, the cycling and empty number placeholders) or disabled, where it marks the control as unavailable: a disabled input, Export PDF before any draw, the text-size buttons at their limits, and the footer actions during a draw (whose icons also dim from Muted to Edge, a second cue besides the text tone). The disabled draw button is the exception: its label explains the state (Drawing, Pool complete, Apply settings to draw), so it uses Muted text, and it is told apart from the enabled button by losing its Primary fill. Hover and selection states keep AA as well: Primary text never sits on a Muted fill (4.4:1). The primary button and the pressed Launch display button turn to an Edge fill with a Muted border on hover, Apply settings gains a Muted ring, and selected text is Canvas on Primary.

Depth comes from mesh lighting, positioning, edges, and restrained shadows using these tokens. The stage adds no new colors, only these tokens at reduced opacity: the ambient dust is soft Edge and Muted specks, and every twelfth speck is a smaller Primary highlight that never exceeds 60% opacity at its core; the light pool behind the sculpture is Muted fading to Edge at no more than 46% opacity (warmed toward Primary only during the reveal); the reveal sheen on number tiles is Primary at no more than 14% opacity. The dust and the light pool also sit under the stage's own CSS dimming; the sheen belongs to the number tiles, which are never dimmed. Numbers carry the strongest visual emphasis. The secondary Display window contains no app title or branding; Full history uses ordinal draw labels. Flex wrapping centers incomplete rows and adapts to the available canvas width and selected display scale.

## Readability and motion

Controller text can be reduced to 90% or enlarged to 150% in 10% steps. The percentage control returns it to 100%, and the preference is saved locally. Text scaling covers labels, controls, history, and dialogs. It does not change number sizing in the controller preview or secondary display.

Informative text is at least 11 px at 100% controller text: the controller's smallest text-size token is 11 px, and no stylesheet sets a smaller pixel size (a unit test checks both). Hierarchy among small text comes from color, weight, case and letter spacing rather than from sizes below 11 px. The welcome screen's small copy and the display's Full history draw state ("Drawing") are 11 px as well. Informative text meets WCAG AA contrast (4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text) against its background, including where it sits over the stage: measured from screenshots with the sculpture lit behind it, the preview's caption stays above 5:1.

The audience display has a separate 50–200% scale control. Both display views wrap numbers and allow vertical scrolling, with custom scrollbars from the palette.

Aeonik Regular, Medium, and Bold are bundled with the application. CJK glyphs fall back to Windows fonts. The installer also registers the supplied fonts for the current Windows user without overwriting existing Aeonik registrations. The portable app renders from the bundled fonts without requiring that registration.

Three.js is loaded asynchronously. Rendering is capped at 30 frames per second at rest and 60 while the sculpture is moving, at 1.5 device pixel ratio, suspended in a hidden document or offscreen canvas, and rendered without animation for reduced-motion users. Each frame is three draw calls: the light pool, the sculpture, and the dust. The pool covers much of the frame, so it is kept cheap: a disc no larger than the light it visibly casts, shaded per vertex, and drawn first so it writes without blending. When WebGL runs on a software renderer (no GPU acceleration, recognised by the renderer's name), a large canvas renders at most about 1.2 million pixels and the browser scales it up, so a 1080p display renders the stage at 1460×821. The controller preview fades the canvas's last 48 px with a panel-coloured gradient laid over it rather than a CSS mask, which would cost the compositor an offscreen pass on every frame.

Frame rates were measured headless under SwiftShader, Chromium's CPU renderer. It exaggerates fill, compositing and shader-compilation costs, so these are worst cases, not GPU figures; each is compared with the previous version on the same machine. The controller at 1440×900 idles and rests at 30 and draws at 59–60 (55–57 at normal process priority, where the previous version held 60). Its stage costs about 14 ms a frame during a draw (previously 11) and 5 ms at rest (previously 4). A 1080p display idles and rests at about 30 and draws at 27–28, as before: there a draw is bound by the software renderer, not the 60 fps budget. At rest beside results its stage costs about 27 ms a frame (previously 18 at full resolution), within the 33 ms budget. A 4K display, which renders the stage at 1920×1080, idles at about 14. After a reveal the stage keeps its 60 fps budget for about 2.2 seconds in the controller and 4.5 in a 1080p display, then returns to 30. The first reveal in each window stalls frames for 170–250 ms (50–100 ms without the tile sheen); later reveals cost the same as without it. That stall is the GPU process compiling the shaders that draw the sheen; the page's main thread does no extra work, and SwiftShader compiles shaders on the CPU. On a GPU it should be far shorter, but that has not been measured. A static CSS sculpture remains if WebGL is unavailable or its context is lost. Each window creates one renderer and reuses it, so returning from the welcome screen never recompiles the scene or flashes the fallback; observers are released when a stage unmounts. Number results keep a stable reading plane.

Drawing uses the selected duration (1–30 seconds, initially 1.6 seconds) of rapid 2D number cycling, followed by a staggered spring reveal. As each tile springs in, one diagonal sheen of Primary light crosses it once (720 ms, starting 0.12 s after that tile's own spring delay). The sheen is a pseudo-element behind the digits, moved by transform alone, so the numbers never move or blur, and it never runs while numbers cycle. It plays only for a reveal the window watched, detected the same way as the stage's light sweep, so restored results and reopening the display never replay it. Switching between Current draw and Full history makes the window forget the reveal, even mid-sheen, so the grids that the switch remounts spring in again without a sheen. Draws of more than 40 numbers skip it to keep the reveal cheap: each sheening tile becomes its own clipped compositing layer while its sheen plays, and from the seventh tile on the tiles share one delay, so their sheens all start together. The depth treatment does not turn this into a slot-machine animation. Reduced-motion preferences suppress decorative movement, entrance animation and the sheen, using a quiet placeholder during the draw before showing the committed result.

The [verification notes](verification.md) distinguish completed checks from acceptance work for the updated interface.

## v1.3.1

The audience Display now shares the locally rendered 3D sculpture, behind current results and full history. Its decorative layer stays fixed while numbers scroll, uses the existing palette, and inherits reduced-motion and WebGL fallback support. Fresh sessions without saved data start at 100 total samples and 10 winners per draw. Existing saved configuration remains unchanged.

## Session reports, 1.4.0

The history sidebar offers a quiet Export PDF action. Reports use A4 portrait pages, the same five-color palette, embedded Aeonik typography, a compact dark masthead, and centered result chips on a light background. Summary figures appear once; continued pages retain session identification, draw headings, and page numbering. All report timestamps explicitly use UTC.

The built-in guide uses plain descriptive section titles without numeric prefixes in all three languages. Session guidance explains when and how to export before resetting.

A separate [Canva design reference](https://canva.link/g9sxk3xfj3dk8f5) documents the report direction. Runtime exports are generated locally and do not depend on Canva or an internet connection.

## Cinematic stage, 1.5.0

The controller now begins at an optional full-window welcome screen. The operator can enter with sound or silently, drag the sculpture, skip the short entrance with Escape, and save a preference to bypass the welcome screen at later starts. The footer retains a Welcome action so revisiting the scene never changes the raffle session.

Since 1.5.1 the stage uses the original lightweight Three.js shader sculpture, generated at runtime with no model download; the 1.5.0 orbital GLB asset was removed. Three.js loads after the controller is available, and a CSS fallback covers devices without WebGL. The same scene appears behind display numbers without display branding. It is dimmed when results are present, reacts to the active draw's duration, and sweeps a restrained band of light across the form only after a live draw completes. Pixel density is capped at 1.5×; hidden or offscreen canvases pause.

The local Web Audio score has a welcome motif, low ambience, duration-aware draw tension, a short pre-reveal breath, and a layered reveal chord. It is produced by a single controller-owned engine, so the display does not echo it. Operators can mute it instantly, adjust Music and Effects independently, and audition it through Test sound. No audio asset, network request, or third-party track is used.

## Stage choreography

The sculpture now takes part in every draw instead of only stepping aside. Its position, scale and motion live in the 3D scene (`src/lib/stage-renderer.ts`), so tucking it beside results no longer scales the canvas as a bitmap and the render stays sharp. CSS only decides how strongly it reads.

The sculpture never floats on a flat background. A soft pool of light sits behind it and follows it wherever the layout moves it. About 280 specks of dust fill the frame in a shallow volume around it and drift slowly, with nearer specks shifting more against the pointer. Specks that could pass in front of the form clear its outline as the camera sees it (the knot's full reach, enlarged by perspective), so they never read as marks on its surface. In the controller preview the canvas stops above the baseline row and fades out over its last 48 px, so the pool, the dust and a tucked sculpture never end in a straight cut.

| Phase | Sculpture | Space around it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Idle | Centered, gently drifting; lifted slightly in the controller preview so it clears the heading | The light pool rests at its lowest strength; the dust drifts slowly |
| Drawing | Comes forward (opacity 0.6 controller, 0.5 display), spins up on an ease-in curve across the selected duration, and its silhouette catches more light; the tube surface breathes with a slow ripple | The camera eases toward the form, up to 7% closer at full energy, so the form grows without leaving its place on screen; the dust drifts faster and brighter and the light pool rises with the same energy |
| Breath | In the last 12% of the draw (at most 240 ms), the same silent breath the score takes before its reveal chord, the energy eases down to 80% of its level when the breath began (0.8 for a draw at full energy) and the spin stops advancing, so the form coasts and slows instead of accelerating into the reveal | The dolly, dust and light pool ease back slightly with the energy, at every draw duration |
| Reveal | One band of light sweeps across the form in 1.1 s with a brief brightening. The spin stays held, so the form settles instead of speeding up again. Nothing changes size, so there is no post-draw pop | The light pool brightens briefly; the dust is pushed gently outward from the form, held for 0.42 s, then drifts back; the camera eases back out |
| Rest | After the sweep, recedes toward the right edge and dims to 0.28 (display 0.26) behind the results | The light pool follows the form; the stage's dimming quiets the pool and the dust with it |

The score and the stage share one definition of the breath and one reveal detector (`src/lib/stage-motion.ts`), so the chord, the light sweep and the dust push always fire together, and never for a result restored from history. Every value eases with frame-rate independent damping, so the motion feels the same on 60, 100 and 144 Hz screens. Layout is always measured from the camera's resting distance, and the dolly moves the camera along its line of sight to the form, so neither the layout target nor the tucked form's place on screen moves during a draw. Rendering keeps the 60 fps budget only while something moves visibly; once the dust's return drifts no faster than its ambient motion, it finishes at 30. Reduced-motion users see the same layout and a static dust field, without suspense, dolly, sweep, push, parallax or travel. The audience display renders a finer mesh for large screens.

### Physical material experiment

Adding `?stage=physical` to the URL swaps the palette shader for a clear-coated `MeshPhysicalMaterial`, so the two looks can be compared side by side; for example, open `/?window=display&stage=physical` in the browser preview next to a default window. The flag works only in the browser preview: the desktop app opens its windows at fixed addresses with no address bar. In the preview, Launch Display carries the flag from the controller to the display window. The flag is read once, when the window's renderer is created, and the default build never loads the extra code. The material is lit by Three's procedural `RoomEnvironment`, prefiltered locally with `PMREMGenerator`, so it needs no HDR download. The prefiltered map is assigned to the material itself, because Three applies a material's reflection strength only to its own map. Its body is tinted Edge, its specular highlights Primary, and its emissive glow Primary. It uses the finer 256 × 32 mesh in every window, because a clear coat shows facets that the palette shader hides.

The experiment has limits. The travelling sweep band becomes an even brightening of the whole form, the surface ripple is absent, and suspense shows as stronger reflections (reflection strength rises from 0.8 at rest to 1.25 at full energy) instead of a brighter rim. Physical shading is also heavier, and its cost grows with the window. Under software rendering (SwiftShader) it idled at about 8 fps on the full-window welcome screen at 1440×900, 23 in the controller preview, and 9 in a 1080p display (rendered at 1460×821); the palette shader idles at 30 in all three. Building the environment also adds a 270–300 ms task when the window opens. After a lost WebGL context, the environment map is regenerated. If the environment cannot be built when the window opens, the window keeps the palette shader. The palette shader remains the default and the reference look.
